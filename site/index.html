Know this before reading an engine permit · checked 07 October 2026

“Emergency” does not mean a fixed number of hours.

The cited federal rule sets no time limit for genuine emergency operation. Its testing allowance is a separate category. This does not give an operator permission to ignore applicable emissions limits.

The hours, the limits, and the questions to ask
  • Emergency operation: no numerical time cap under 40 CFR 60.4211(f)(1).
  • Maintenance and readiness testing: generally 100 hours per calendar year under this rule, with specified provisions for additional testing hours.
  • Other allowed non-emergency operation: up to 50 hours within—not in addition to—the 100 hours, with restrictions on use and narrow exceptions. This is not unrestricted commercial generation.

Read the federal rule, §60.4211(f) · Same provision in the Jasper PDF, page 56

Jasper County: 500 hours is not a blanket allowance

The technical support document says the site cannot operate every emergency generator for 500 hours per year under its stated emissions constraints. The draft’s critical-generator caps are 230 tons NOx, 55 tons VOC and 210 tons CO per rolling 12 months; §D.1.1 does not state an emergency exemption to these caps. These annual totals are not short-term exposure limits. TSD, PDF page 154 · Draft caps, PDF page 27.

Ask for accountability during an outage

What qualifies as an emergency, and what evidence records its start and end? How many engines may operate together? How are startup and emergency emissions counted toward the caps? What protects nearby residents during a prolonged outage? Request event logs, fuel use, control-system status, emissions calculations and an explanation of any claimed exemption.

This explains the cited rule and reviewed draft, not proof of misuse or a universal exemption across all permits. Check the final permit and applicable requirements for each project.

How should a community measure this?
  1. Establish the baseline. Record air quality, population health and other pollution sources before construction; retain a suitable comparison area.
  2. Count attributable emissions. Separate power generation from on-site engines; use operating hours, fuel and controls, not permitted maximums as actual emissions.
  3. Model exposure and outcomes. Include downwind communities, baseline disease rates and uncertainty. Report cases and dollars, geography, study year and dollar year.
  4. Keep the accounts separate. Medical spending, productivity and broader health valuations can overlap. Do not add them without checking the model’s definitions; keep climate damages and public-budget costs distinct.

Local estimate: not yet available. National studies cannot be divided by the number of facilities to produce a credible neighborhood bill. Noise and water-related health burdens require separate assessment.

Who should pay? A policy proposal

Require operators to fund independent baseline monitoring, emissions reduction and follow-up assessment. Publish attributable costs and proposed funding for residual harm, including affected downwind communities. Any fees or compensation need a lawful, transparent basis; payments do not replace protective limits.

Report tax revenue and public spending in a fiscal account, and health damages in a social-cost account. Tax receipts alone do not establish that health burdens have been offset.

How to measure climate and health correctly

Climate inventory: sum electricity used in each interval × a matching grid emission factor; add onsite fuel, refrigerant leaks and lifecycle emissions within a stated boundary. Report operational annual emissions and embodied emissions separately. Avoid counting cooling electricity twice when facility power already includes it. Keep certificate-based claims distinct from physical grid emissions. [GHG]

Impact of a new project: compare the electricity system with and without the project, using marginal dispatch and future capacity scenarios. Average grid factors describe attribution; they do not establish the incremental consequence of a new campus.

Health chain: emissions → concentration change → exposed population and baseline disease → epidemiological response → health outcomes and valuation. EPA COBRA is a screening tool for the contiguous US; local dispersion and country-specific population, mortality and atmospheric data may be needed. Low/high estimates reflect alternative health functions, not automatically a 95% confidence interval. [EPA]

Keep the dates straight: the original Han paper’s 2030 scenario reported 940–1,590 modeled annual deaths (about 1,300 rounded). Version 4 instead reports 947–1,576 for its high-demand 2028 scenario. These are modeled impacts of all data centers, not observed deaths or AI-only totals. [HAN]

Heat, noise and water stress deserve separate assessment. A low carbon footprint does not prove low public-health impact.

Income category is context, not a risk rating. Emerging economies can host cleaner projects; industrialized economies can have severe local constraints. UNCTAD highlights unequal resource and waste burdens across the digital supply chain. [UN]

National permits: a sequence with stop points
  1. Siting: assess the whole campus and its cumulative grid, air and water impacts; compare alternatives and consult affected communities.
  2. Construction: approve a bounded phase with enforceable resource limits, monitoring, funding and closure security.
  3. Energization: verify power delivery, water availability, pollution controls and emergency plans before each load increment.
  4. Operations: publish measured performance, enforce exceedance remedies and reopen review for expansion or material changes.

Proposed national minimum disclosure and protection rules should complement regional grid and watershed decisions and meaningful local participation. Review linked facilities together so developers cannot evade scrutiny by splitting a campus into smaller applications.

International rules: prevent exported harm

Proposed international cooperation should establish comparable facility reporting, lifecycle accounting, cross-border electricity and pollution assessment, equipment traceability and independent verification. National authorities would issue permits under domestic law; this guide does not propose an existing global permit agency.

Multinational operators and wealthier consumer markets should help finance monitoring capacity, cleaner infrastructure and safe equipment recovery in lower-income host and supplier countries. Lending and public procurement can make these safeguards contractual. Local communities need accessible grievance and remedy processes.

Existing examples: EU Regulation 2024/1364 establishes reporting for facilities with installed IT power demand of at least 500 kW; reporting is not environmental clearance. Singapore’s 2024 roadmap links additional capacity to efficiency and green energy. These are examples to adapt, not evidence that a universal sustainability threshold exists. [EU] [SG]

Before the numbers

What kind of data center are we talking about?

A data center brings computers, storage and networking together with power, cooling and security. It can serve an organization’s own needs or supply computing to others. These labels describe different aspects of a facility:

Enterprise
An organization operates infrastructure for its own services, such as business systems or research.
Colocation
Customers place their equipment in shared premises. The operator supplies space and supporting infrastructure.
Cloud
A provider makes computing resources available remotely. This describes how a service is delivered.
Hyperscale
Large, scalable computing operations. This describes scale; it is not a universal server-count threshold or a synonym for every cloud facility.
Edge
Computing located closer to users or devices to reduce communication delay. Location alone does not establish a small environmental footprint.
Campus
A project site that may contain multiple buildings and development phases. One map marker need not mean one operating building.

Definitions: AWS infrastructure explainer and LBNL’s 2024 U.S. report. Ask which buildings, phases and equipment a project’s numbers include.

Is a data center the same thing as AI?

No. Data centers also support email, storage, business software, science and other digital services. AI includes systems that recognize patterns, make predictions or generate content. Training develops a model; inference uses it. Both can require electricity, and their shares vary with the service and how much it is used. Large models often use data centers, but some inference runs on phones or personal computers. That moves part of the work; it does not erase the device’s energy or manufacturing footprint. Inference energy research; documented on-device and server models.

Does more efficient AI mean a smaller total footprint?

It can reduce electricity per useful task, but total use also depends on how many tasks are performed, output quality, equipment and the power supply. Illustrative arithmetic: halving energy per task while tripling the number of tasks increases task electricity by 50%. Measure absolute energy, emissions and water alongside efficiency. Compare equal service quality and include equipment manufacturing when making a lifecycle claim. Green AI review; computing lifecycle research.

How should I read a project map or filing?

Check whether the record describes a zoning request, a proposed campus, a permitted component, construction or actual operations. A generator permit does not establish that an entire campus is operating. Generator capacity, computer capacity and total facility demand measure different things. In Project watch, inspect the document type, project phase, source and measurement boundary before comparing numbers. An opposition comment records a concern; establishing the impact requires supporting evidence.

Where do the numbers come from?

The denominator is the average monthly residential electricity consumption in EIA Table 5a, 2024 data (released October 7, 2025; checked October 1, 2026). Published values are rounded to whole kWh. Multiplying by 12 provides an annual comparison benchmark, not a seasonal household demand profile.

Capacity mode: annual MWh = facility MW × average fraction of capacity × hours in the selected year (8,760 normally; 8,784 in a leap year). Annual-total mode uses the entered MWh directly. Residential-customer equivalents = annual MWh × 1,000 ÷ (state monthly kWh × 12). No PUE factor is added to total-facility inputs.

      Data centers, health and responsibility

      Download cited review

      Structured abstract

      Background. Data centers connect digital innovation to electricity, water, materials and local infrastructure. Their footprint varies with workloads, cooling technology, electricity supply and the location of affected communities. Environmental responsibility therefore requires more than a favorable power-usage-effectiveness ratio or an annual renewable-energy claim.

      Objective. Map published scientific evidence and selected public records; identify measurable indicators for U.S. state comparison; investigate Indiana water claims and local resistance; and define conditions under which additional useful computing could remain within an explicit carbon budget.

      Methods. A rapid structured web search and source-following exercise was conducted on 1 October 2026. The included evidence register contains 86 records, including a 23-record core literature corpus of varied publication types. Public-source evidence adds a 50-state electricity model, reported water balances for 23 Google campuses in 14 states, and selected local governance documents. Sources were charted by geography, period, boundary, method and limitations. No exhaustive subscription-database search, independent duplicate screening or meta-analysis was performed.

      Results. Electricity use, direct water loss, upstream water, embodied emissions, air pollution and noise need separate indicators. EPRI’s 2026 model places U.S. data-center-plus-cryptocurrency electricity at 184.203 TWh in 2024; this is an estimate, not a utility census. Its Indiana midrange value rises from 0.820 TWh in 2024 to 27.595 TWh in the conditional 2030 medium scenario. These are not AI-only figures. Local Indiana evidence shows why annual projections, shared water-system limits, sewer design and construction dewatering cannot be treated as measured campus consumption. [25] [45] [46] [47] [56]

      Conclusion. Efficiency can enable more useful computing, but a lower footprint per task does not guarantee lower total emissions. A defensible no-increase claim requires a defined counterfactual, absolute emissions accounting, workload-quality controls, additional clean supply where appropriate, and protections against transferring impacts to water-stressed or highly exposed communities. This deliverable is a scoping-review foundation, not a claim to have identified all published literature. [9] [7]

      1. Review questions and methods

      The review asks four questions: (1) which direct and indirect environmental-health pathways are supported by the literature; (2) which metrics are comparable across states and which require facility or watershed resolution; (3) what the Indiana evidence establishes about water use and resistance; and (4) how AI deployment and data-intensive innovation could be constrained by absolute carbon, water and exposure budgets. Global scientific studies inform mechanisms; U.S. sources inform the state map; Indiana receives a purposive case study.

      Eligibility included scholarly research, reviews and relevant commentaries available by 1 October 2026; authoritative technical inventories and methodological standards; corporate disclosures with identifiable periods and boundaries; and dated government records or original local reporting needed to establish policy events. English-language accessible records were prioritized. General ICT estimates were used only where their boundaries could be distinguished from data centers. Unsupported social-media estimates, generic commercial summaries and duplicate preprint/published versions were not treated as independent evidence.

      Searches used the built-in web search to locate publisher pages, DOI records, PubMed records, author manuscripts, institutional repositories, national-laboratory reports, regulator websites and municipal records. The supplied search-log download preserves query strings from the scholarly, state, Indiana and methods searches. A representative executed query was “Mytton 2021 Data centre water consumption npj Clean Water”; state and policy searches combined named jurisdictions with water, data centers, moratorium, ordinance and official-site terms. These were targeted queries rather than a reproducible exhaustive database export.

      Selection was performed by one automated research workflow with parallel thematic extraction and a subsequent targeted consistency audit. This is not independent duplicate human screening. The workflow deduplicated bibliography identities by DOI, title and URL during integration. The log records selected consolidation and exclusion decisions, but transient search snippets and a complete denominator of all screened records were not preserved. Accordingly, no invented PRISMA identification, exclusion or eligibility totals are shown. The included-record count describes the included register, not all records identified. [58]

      Data charting retained authors, year, publication type, geography, methods, findings, units, accounting boundaries, status and limitations. Access varied from full reports and manuscripts to abstracts or indexed government text; these limits are recorded. Critical appraisal was descriptive: direct metering or measurement versus engineering estimate or model, temporal relevance, transparency of assumptions, spatial resolution, corporate interest, completeness and support for causality. No formal intervention risk-of-bias scale or pooled certainty grade was assigned to incompatible study designs.

      Synthesis is narrative and stratified by outcome and evidence type. Electricity forecasts are not pooled with observations; corporate campus records are not extrapolated to entire states; municipal rules are not promoted into statewide law. “Not established” denotes a retrieval or evidence gap, not proof of zero impact. Statistical meta-analysis would be misleading across different denominators, workloads, grid years, time horizons and lifecycle boundaries.

      3. Scientific evidence and critical synthesis

      What the literature establishes

      Datacenter impacts are measurable, geographically uneven and sensitive to accounting boundaries. Energy efficiency is consequential, but an improved ratio does not establish an absolute reduction. Masanet et al. (2020; L01) modeled 205 TWh of global datacenter electricity in 2018, 6% above 2010 despite 550% growth in compute instances. This is evidence that efficiency and infrastructure changes can decouple service growth from electricity growth over a specified historical interval. It should not be used as a current consumption estimate or proof that subsequent AI expansion will remain decoupled.

      Different published totals can also share the same underlying assumptions. Mytton and Ashtine (2022; L11) traced 258 energy estimates from 46 original publications through 676 sources. Their provenance review makes a methodological point for the dashboard: display original sources and dates, distinguish measured electricity from modeled stock estimates, and do not treat repetitions of one estimate as independent corroboration. Counts of proposed facilities and interconnection requests are not operational electricity consumption.

      Water accounting requires an additional spatial boundary. Mytton (2021; L02) distinguishes facility water from electricity-related water and explains why site WUE alone is incomplete. Water withdrawal is intake; consumptive use is the portion not returned to the relevant water system. Neither permits nor utility service capacity demonstrate actual consumptive use. A locally meaningful inventory needs withdrawals, returns, sources, seasonal peaks and watershed conditions, with the WUE denominator stated explicitly.

      Siddik, Shehabi and Marston (2021; L03) linked a 2018 U.S. datacenter inventory to electricity and utility systems. Approximately one-fifth of the direct water footprint originated in moderately or highly stressed watersheds; nearly half of servers relied partly or wholly on power plants in stressed regions. Their operational model excludes manufacturing and is not a present-day facility census. Nevertheless, it explains why state borders alone cannot contain a facility's environmental footprint: electricity imports move part of the water and carbon burden elsewhere.

      Li et al. (2025; L05) estimate 5.4 million liters of operational water for a GPT-3 training example, including 0.7 million liters on site. Those are modeled quantities, not disclosed training-run water meters. The published CACM article supersedes the 2023 preprint for bibliography purposes. Its carbon–water argument also matters operationally: choosing the lowest-carbon hour or region does not necessarily select the lowest-water option. Universal claims that a prompt always consumes a fixed bottle fraction erase model, output-length, grid and cooling differences.

      AI, infrastructure and the life cycle

      Task-level benchmarks support using the smallest suitable system, while imposing limits on comparisons. Luccioni, Jernite and Strubell (2024; L06) report mean electricity per 1,000 inferences of 0.002 kWh for text classification, 0.047 for text generation and 2.907 for image generation. Their text-generation benchmark emits only 10 new tokens per input. These values are useful illustrations of workload variation, not current universal chatbot coefficients or proof of equivalent task quality. Procurement benchmarks should compare systems on the same task, output requirements, error tolerance and complete service boundary.

      Training-only carbon ledgers are incomplete. Luccioni, Viguier and Ligozat (2023; L04) estimate 24.7 tonnes CO2e for BLOOM's final training dynamic electricity, versus 50.5 tonnes when adding the study's broader equipment and operational allocation. Gupta et al. (2021; L19) similarly show why manufacturing and infrastructure deserve attention as operational carbon decreases. Their relative contributions vary with device, service lifetime and electricity supply; neither paper licenses one embodied-carbon percentage for every facility.

      Material throughput creates another boundary. Wang et al. (2024; L10) project 1.2–5.0 million tonnes of cumulative generative-AI e-waste during 2020–2030, with 16–86% reductions under circular-economy scenarios. The range is conditional, not an observed annual waste total. Longer useful life, redeployment and documented downstream treatment therefore belong beside operational efficiency in a responsibility assessment. A hardware upgrade requires comparing avoided operational emissions with the new equipment's manufacturing burden.

      The research literature offers methods, not guaranteed savings. Henderson et al. (2020; L17) develop instrumented energy/carbon reporting; Strubell et al. (2019; L16) draw attention to development and tuning costs; Schwartz et al. (2020; L08) argue for efficiency alongside accuracy. Dodge et al. (2022; L18) demonstrate operational-carbon variation across cloud regions and times. Together they support measuring full workloads before scheduling, caching, batching, quantizing or replacing models; they do not establish that every optimization preserves quality or decreases total annual demand.

      Environmental health and responsibility

      The datacenter-specific health literature remains much weaker on causal outcomes than the footprint literature. Tao and Gao (2025; L13) is a research-agenda letter; Gour, Ortiz and Maibach (2026; L23) is an exploratory Virginia mini review. Both identify credible exposure pathways and substantial empirical gaps. Neither directly measures disease incidence attributable to a particular site. Indoor occupational noise levels must not be presented as outdoor residential exposure measurements.

      Han et al. (2026; L14) model pollution-related public-health costs and propose health-informed computing. The June 2026 version reports CACM acceptance and an upper projected burden exceeding $20 billion annually in 2028, with about seven times the national household-average burden in the most affected counties. These are modeled outcomes. Earlier versions used different horizons and inequality comparisons; quoting their claims as current would be misleading.

      The most recent health-equity synthesis, Mithu et al. (2026; L22), extends attention to workers, critical-mineral extraction and e-waste communities; only its abstract was assessed here. Ngata et al. (2025; L12) provide a preliminary Northern Virginia community case, while Hogan (2015; L20) examines water and infrastructure governance in Utah. These sources justify evaluating participation, access to records, cost allocation and distribution of burdens alongside technical performance. They cannot establish the current legal status of an Indiana moratorium; that requires dated municipal documents.

      A defensible route to additional computing within a carbon budget

      An absolute emissions constraint is more demanding than a lower footprint per computation. Kaack et al. (2022; L09) distinguish computing impacts, application effects and wider system changes. Consequently, an AI climate-benefit claim needs a specified counterfactual and measured avoided emissions, minus computing, hardware and induced-demand effects. An efficiency gain that lowers costs and expands use can leave total emissions higher. No reviewed work establishes that arbitrary AI growth can occur with literally no environmental footprint.

      Recent reviews underscore implementation gaps. Verdecchia et al. (2023; L07) synthesize 98 Green AI studies, heavily oriented toward training and laboratory work. Chong et al. (2026; L21) review 64 cooling-control studies: only 18 report PUE, three WUE and one CUE. Their reported energy savings cannot be treated as a pooled expected reduction. A local pilot should retain temperature, reliability and water constraints and compare independently metered outcomes against a credible baseline.

      Xiao et al. (2025; L15) combine U.S. server-expansion, cooling and grid scenarios. They identify substantial combined mitigation potential but remaining footprints and difficult siting constraints. Their state rankings depend on future grids and hydropower-water allocation; they are scenarios, not permit recommendations.

      The following dashboard policy is an analytical recommendation from this synthesis: set annual gross operational and lifecycle carbon budgets, plus site-specific seasonal water caps and exposure limits. Publish both annual totals and service-normalized intensities. Track emissions from actual electricity supply separately from contractual renewable claims; document new clean supply and hourly matching where available. Verify that scheduling does not transfer burdens into stressed watersheds or highly exposed communities. Require generator operating hours and emissions, boundary noise monitoring, public complaints and remedial responses. Reserve low-impact computing capacity for applications with demonstrable public value, and reassess rebound as demand grows. A claim of “no increase” is credible only when the complete measured budget remains within its defined baseline.

      The most consequential distinction: water quantities have different boundaries

      The Indiana dashboard should present each water datum with a metric definition, facility or system boundary, time period, phase, and evidence status. Withdrawal is water entering a facility from a source; consumption is the portion not returned to that source over the accounting period. Sewer discharge is an outgoing flow. Storage volume and pumping capability are capacities, not use. Temporary construction dewatering requires its own category. Neither design maxima nor daily peaks can be multiplied by 365 and called observed annual consumption.

      6. Implementation: AI progress within an absolute budget

      The relevant goal is more useful output while holding a defined environmental budget constant. Literal zero-footprint computing is not established by the reviewed literature. A defensible project can instead test whether incremental annual and lifecycle emissions stay below an agreed baseline, with separate water and health constraints. This requires the same service quality, time horizon and system boundary before and after implementation. Any claimed societal emissions savings need a documented counterfactual and should be reported separately from the operator’s gross footprint. [9] [15]

      7. Limitations and research priorities

      This rapid scoping synthesis is not an exhaustive systematic review of all published literature. Retrieval was English-focused and relied on web discoverability and accessible records; some full texts were unavailable. There was no preregistered protocol, librarian-reviewed indexed search, complete screening ledger, independent duplicate appraisal or author contact. Selected sources overlap and some technical totals share underlying models. The included corpus therefore supports a traceable research foundation, not a claim of saturation or a publication-ready PRISMA flow diagram.

      The main state layer is modeled and includes cryptocurrency mining. Water records are a single-operator campus sample, intentionally harmonized to 2024, with possible engineering estimation. Indiana has no verified comprehensive industry consumption total in this extraction. Local policy searches were selective and dates may be superseded. The Boone County announcement initially had a retrieval anomaly; the signed ordinance was subsequently recovered and verified. Health burden estimates are modeled or synthesized, while direct site-specific epidemiology remains limited. Lack of quantified disease burden is not evidence of no health risk. [13] [14] [22] [23] [50]

      To complete a formal systematic review, preregister a protocol; commission a librarian-assisted search of Scopus, Web of Science, PubMed, IEEE Xplore and ACM Digital Library, with appropriate engineering databases and citation chasing; export all records; deduplicate deterministically; use two independent screeners and a dispute process; log full-text exclusion reasons; and use design-appropriate critical appraisal. Extend beyond English where feasible, rerun the search close to publication, and separate endpoint-specific reviews if a meta-analysis becomes possible. A proposed search block is: ("data cent*" OR datacent* OR "AI server*" OR "artificial intelligence") AND (water OR electricity OR carbon OR "life cycle" OR pollution OR noise OR health OR "environmental justice"). This block is a recommendation for future indexed searching, not a claim that it was run across those databases. [58]

      8. Conclusions

      Environmental responsibility is demonstrated by transparent boundaries, measured absolute outcomes, credible counterfactuals and enforceable local protections. A low PUE, an annual renewable purchase or a replenishment promise is insufficient on its own. Indiana’s immediate need is clearer facility-level water accounting and trustworthy governance; the national need is to couple additional computing with real capacity, clean electricity, lifecycle stewardship and community-level exposure evidence. The most supportable ambition is useful AI growth within explicit, verified budgets—not an unqualified promise of no footprint.

      10. Environmental assessment framework for data centers and AI services

      A complete metric framework is not the same as a completed facility assessment. The following twenty indicators specify outcomes, units, boundaries, methods, evidence and limitations. Actual impacts cannot be fully quantified where meter records, supplier inventories, local exposure data or a defensible counterfactual are missing. Missing information remains unknown; it is never assigned a zero or used to certify an environmentally neutral system.

      Define the functional unit before comparison: an accepted service outcome at a specified quality, latency, accessibility and error-risk level. Report both annual absolute totals and intensity per useful outcome. Include training, tuning, inference, retrieval, storage, retries, human correction, networks and devices when material, together with equipment production, facility construction, maintenance and end-of-life. State exclusions and allocation rules explicitly. A California purchaser may rely on facilities in other states; procurement location cannot substitute for hosting geography. [4] [6] [17] [19]

      Assessment has two linked views. Attributional inventories allocate the footprint of existing activities; consequential studies estimate what changes relative to a counterfactual. Annual electricity inventories, market-based contractual claims and hourly marginal grid impacts must remain distinct. Avoid counting both supplier totals and their allocated customer share in the same system total. Assess energy-water tradeoffs, drought and heat-wave coincidence, local pollution, equipment replacement and demand rebound before accepting an efficiency claim. [60] [59] [3] [9] [65]

      Measurement maturity and interpretation

      Established operational measurement. Electricity, flow, fuel and basic noise indicators have established instruments. The main obstacles here are public access, matching boundaries and time resolution.

      Established methods, allocation required. GHG inventories, PUE/WUE and life-cycle accounting are tractable, but comparability depends on definitions, denominator matching, supplier data and uncertainty analysis.

      Local assessment required. Exposure, effluent impacts, hydrology and biodiversity require source-specific field data and models. State averages or general literature cannot substitute for these inputs.

      Counterfactual and distributional analysis. Marginal emissions, net public benefits and AI rebound depend on alternatives and affected populations. Report assumptions and separate indicators rather than a universal score.

      Absolute electricity use — MWh/site/year; kWh/workload

      Purpose and boundary. Absolute demand determines infrastructure scale; an efficient facility can increase electricity use when workloads and installed capacity grow. Operational electricity for the named campus and reporting period; distinguish IT, cooling, power losses and other support loads. [25] [39] [1] [66]

      Measurement. Sum calibrated import and on-site generation meters, reconcile exports and storage, and avoid counting generator electricity twice. Suggested cadence: Retain hourly readings where available, reconcile monthly bills, publish annual totals, and report missing intervals and estimated shares. Minimum evidence: Campus boundary, meter locations, reporting dates, grid imports, generator output, storage treatment, estimation share and utility reconciliation records.

      Decision use. Compare expansion plans with observed demand trajectories and separately assess which operational changes reduce total electricity use over time. Tradeoff or limitation: Reducing cooling energy may increase water consumption; distinguish genuine demand reduction from shifting activity to another facility or supplier. Current coverage: EPRI supplies modeled state electricity, not site meters; literature benchmarks provide selected workload measurements, with no complete campus inventory.

      Peak load, timing and flexibility — MW; hourly MWh; verified curtailed MWh

      Purpose and boundary. Grid capacity and reliability depend on when loads occur, while annual electricity totals can hide concentrated local infrastructure pressure. Facility demand at the grid connection and relevant utility or balancing area, distinguishing contracted, installed and actual coincident peaks. [25] [26] [43] [65]

      Measurement. Use interval meters to identify seasonal peaks; compare dispatch events with documented baselines and report rebound demand after curtailment. Suggested cadence: Archive hourly or finer data, review monthly peaks, and assess summer, winter and grid-stress events separately from annual consumption. Minimum evidence: Interval load series, timezone, interconnection boundary, seasonal system peaks, baseline method, dispatched reductions, generator operation and subsequent recovery load.

      Decision use. Evaluate interconnection sizing, demand response and workload scheduling with utility-specific constraints and tested performance rather than promised flexible capacity. Tradeoff or limitation: Shifting computation may reduce peak demand but move water use, latency or emissions to a different hour or location. Current coverage: EPRI includes modeled peak GW and scenarios; this dataset lacks metered hourly loads, coincident utility peaks and demonstrated flexibility.

      Facility overhead efficiency: PUE — Dimensionless total facility energy / IT energy

      Purpose and boundary. PUE isolates support overhead, enabling cooling and electrical-loss diagnosis while leaving workload usefulness and total demand to separate metrics. Facility and IT energy measured over identical periods and declared measurement points; include all relevant energy inputs consistently. [66] [39] [1]

      Measurement. Divide facility energy by IT energy, disclose metering category and weather/utilization context, and retain the absolute numerator and denominator. Suggested cadence: Report annual energy-weighted PUE alongside monthly values; investigate seasonal changes and alterations in occupancy, IT loading or cooling operation. Minimum evidence: Facility and IT energy meters, synchronized periods, metering category, allocation for shared buildings, occupancy, weather and documented calculation method.

      Decision use. Use PUE to compare operational improvements within compatible boundaries, paired with absolute electricity, water and useful-service output measures. Tradeoff or limitation: Improved PUE can accompany greater evaporation or more wasteful IT activity; it does not establish lower total environmental burden. Current coverage: The dataset explains PUE but lacks matched facility and IT meter series for Indiana or California project-level comparisons.

      Purchased-electricity GHG inventory — tCO2e/year; gCO2e/kWh, separately by method

      Purpose and boundary. Two accounting views describe physical grid context and contractual procurement; annual renewable claims alone cannot demonstrate carbon-free electricity every hour. Purchased electricity within the reported campus or organization; keep location-based and market-based inventories separate and disclose geographic matching. [60] [61] [59]

      Measurement. Multiply electricity by documented grid-average factors for location accounting; apply qualifying contractual instruments separately, preserving residual-mix and factor provenance. Suggested cadence: Publish annual inventories with factor vintage and contractual coverage; add hourly matching diagnostics when time-resolved energy and supply exist. Minimum evidence: Electricity totals, factor geography and year, gases and GWP version, instrument ownership and retirement, coverage gaps and accounting rules.

      Decision use. Assess inventory disclosure and procurement quality without subtracting claimed avoided emissions or treating certificates as proof of local zero emissions. Tradeoff or limitation: Contractual reductions may differ from local hourly emissions changes; renewable procurement also has land, materials and water consequences elsewhere. Current coverage: Existing GHG guidance supports methods; no matched campus load, factors and instruments are available here to calculate audited site inventories.

      Consequential electricity emissions — Change in tCO2e versus explicit counterfactual

      Purpose and boundary. A decision can change emissions differently from its allocated inventory, especially when new capacity or different generating units respond. Power-system response to an identified load, siting, scheduling or procurement decision, over a stated geography and time horizon. [65] [61] [9]

      Measurement. Model incremental generation and capacity changes against an explicit baseline; label marginal factors, dispatch assumptions and sensitivity ranges. Suggested cadence: Assess each material decision; update scenarios when grid forecasts, load shape or transmission assumptions change, retaining previous versions for comparison. Minimum evidence: Counterfactual, decision horizon, hourly loads, marginal-emissions model, capacity response, transmission constraints, model vintage and transparent sensitivity analysis.

      Decision use. Compare alternatives with common service requirements and uncertainty ranges; report consequential results separately from location-based and market-based inventories. Tradeoff or limitation: Short-run dispatch savings may conflict with long-run infrastructure effects, local air pollution or water demand; scenario choices influence conclusions. Current coverage: Marginal-emissions preprint offers methods; existing state scenarios do not identify causal grid responses or establish site-specific avoided emissions.

      Embodied and full life-cycle GHG — tCO2e/project; kgCO2e/defined service

      Purpose and boundary. Operational decarbonization leaves equipment and construction burdens; omitting manufacturing can substantially understate the footprint of a specific AI deployment. Raw materials, fabrication, buildings, equipment, transport, operation and end-of-life; identify networks and user devices included or excluded explicitly. [4] [19] [67] [10]

      Measurement. Combine process inventories with operational data; allocate shared hardware transparently across service life and utilization, testing alternative lifetimes and allocations. Suggested cadence: Update at procurement, retrofit and retirement, then reconcile annually with asset records and significant changes in utilization or supplier data. Minimum evidence: Bill of materials, supplier inventories, asset lifetimes, utilization, functional unit, allocation rules, electricity assumptions, end-of-life treatment and uncertainty ranges.

      Decision use. Compare replacement, refurbishment and deployment options using equivalent service requirements, consistent system boundaries and separate life-cycle stage results. Tradeoff or limitation: New hardware can reduce operational energy yet increase manufacturing impacts; lifetime extension benefits depend on performance, reliability and grid emissions. Current coverage: BLOOM and computing studies illustrate allocated life-cycle estimates; project bills of materials and comparable supplier inventories are absent here.

      Direct fuel and refrigerant emissions — Fuel quantity; kg refrigerant lost; tCO2e/year

      Purpose and boundary. Backup generation and cooling-fluid leaks remain direct emissions sources even when annual purchased electricity is matched with renewable instruments. On-site combustion and refrigerant releases from campus equipment, including maintenance and retirement; separate direct emissions from purchased electricity. [66] [68] [28]

      Measurement. Calculate combustion from fuel records and factors; estimate refrigerant losses through charge, additions, recovery and inventory reconciliation by substance. Suggested cadence: Record every refueling, refrigerant service and leak event; reconcile annual stocks and report testing, outage and routine operating hours. Minimum evidence: Fuel invoices, engine runtime, refrigerant species and charge, service additions, recovered mass, emission factors, GWP version and boundary reconciliation.

      Decision use. Prioritize leak prevention and low-emission backup options; assess actual use separately from installed engine capacity and maximum permitted operation. Tradeoff or limitation: Air-cooled or mechanical cooling may reduce water demand while changing electricity use and refrigerant exposure; backup choices change reliability and pollution. Current coverage: Existing sources describe designs and reporting concepts; no complete campus fuel ledger, runtime history or refrigerant mass balance is included.

      Direct withdrawal by source — m³/year; m³/day; source-specific volumes

      Purpose and boundary. Withdrawal indicates pressure on supply and infrastructure, but does not reveal how much returns or whether the source is locally scarce. Water entering the named campus from surface water, groundwater, municipal or reclaimed supplies; report construction dewatering separately from operations. [2] [28] [42] [45] [56]

      Measurement. Meter each intake, identify ultimate source and treatment, and distinguish withdrawals from storage capacity, sewer flows and water consumed. Suggested cadence: Publish monthly totals, daily peaks and seasonal source mix; retain shorter intervals during drought, construction pumping or supply restrictions. Minimum evidence: Intake meters, source identities, daily and monthly series, construction versus operation separation, estimation flags and utility delivery reconciliations.

      Decision use. Assess water-supply agreements and drought performance using actual demand by source, independently from treatment plant capacity and permit design maxima. Tradeoff or limitation: Reclaimed water can reduce potable demand while altering treatment energy and downstream return flows; source substitution requires a watershed-specific assessment. Current coverage: Google discloses selected 2024 campus volumes, sometimes estimated; the sample contains no Indiana or California campus water measurements.

      Direct consumption and stated WUE — m³/year; L/kWh IT, numerator explicitly labeled

      Purpose and boundary. Consumptive losses reduce water available to other users; WUE supports operational diagnosis only when its numerator and denominator are consistent. Water not returned within the stated basin and time boundary; distinguish site water-input WUE from consumption-based intensity measures. [2] [28] [66]

      Measurement. Reconcile withdrawals, discharge destinations and storage changes; divide the explicitly named water numerator by matched IT electricity for intensity. Suggested cadence: Reconcile monthly water balances and publish annual WUE with summer peaks; separately report uncertain returns, storage changes and measurement gaps. Minimum evidence: Withdrawal, discharge and storage records, basin and timing of returns, IT energy, balance closure, water quality and explicitly stated WUE definition.

      Decision use. Compare cooling alternatives using both absolute volumes and defined intensities; do not calculate WUE from state electricity and unrelated campuses. Tradeoff or limitation: Low site WUE can shift demand to electricity generation; low consumption does not imply harmless discharge temperature or chemistry. Current coverage: Google provides withdrawal-minus-discharge estimates for named sites; missing matched IT electricity prevents comparable campus WUE calculations in this dataset.

      Indirect electricity and supply-chain water — m³ withdrawal and m³ consumption, separately

      Purpose and boundary. Cooling alone misses water used by power plants and semiconductor production, potentially shifting burdens to distant communities and ecosystems. Electricity-generation and hardware-manufacturing water attributed to the service; retain upstream watersheds and distinguish these burdens from direct site water. [3] [5] [15] [67]

      Measurement. Multiply location-specific energy or material activity by compatible withdrawal and consumption factors; disclose cooling, hydropower and allocation assumptions. Suggested cadence: Recalculate annually and when electricity sourcing, hardware procurement or operating location changes; use seasonal factors where validated data permit. Minimum evidence: Energy geography, generation mix, water-factor definitions and vintage, supplier process data, basin assignments, allocation method and uncertainty ranges.

      Decision use. Evaluate cooling and procurement together, reporting direct and indirect components and testing alternative electricity-water factors before selecting locations. Tradeoff or limitation: Water-intensive clean electricity or fabrication can offset site savings; upstream allocation choices can dominate cross-location comparisons and apparent rankings. Current coverage: Studies offer modeled indirect-water examples; supplier inventories and matched campus electricity are missing, preventing reliable project-specific totals here.

      Seasonal scarcity and supply resilience — Monthly m³; basin stress category; drought scenarios

      Purpose and boundary. Equal annual volumes can create different harms depending on dry-season demand, groundwater recovery, existing users and ecological flow requirements. Facility and upstream watersheds, local users and ecological flows during relevant months; distinguish water availability from treatment and pipe capacity. [62] [3] [43] [47]

      Measurement. Overlay source-specific seasonal demand with basin screening indicators, validated local hydrology and drought scenarios; avoid interpreting screening scores as spare supply. Suggested cadence: Review seasonal conditions monthly, update drought assessments as conditions change, and reassess before expansions or changes in water source. Minimum evidence: Source watershed, monthly withdrawals and consumption, competing demand, groundwater or streamflow data, drought assumptions, ecological requirements and local hydrologic review.

      Decision use. Screen sites, then request local drought-yield analysis, operating triggers and contingency plans before concluding that infrastructure can support expansion. Tradeoff or limitation: Lower-carbon locations may face greater water scarcity; annual replenishment claims may occur in different basins or seasons from consumption. Current coverage: Aqueduct supplies screening context; Indiana system capacities do not establish sustainable yield, and this dataset lacks calibrated campus water models.

      Combustion pollution and population exposure — kg NOx/PM2.5; µg/m³; separately modeled health outcomes

      Purpose and boundary. Pollution burdens depend on where emissions travel and who is exposed; equal emissions totals can produce unequal potential health impacts. On-site engines plus attributed power-plant pollution and exposed receptors; separate emitted mass, modeled concentration, observed exposure and health outcomes. [68] [13] [14] [22] [23]

      Measurement. Combine runtime and tested or factor-based emissions with dispersion and population data; validate concentrations and retain uncertainty in health models. Suggested cadence: Log engine events continuously, summarize emissions annually, and sample relevant background, testing and outage periods with meteorology and receptor locations. Minimum evidence: Engine specifications, fuel and hours, stack tests or factor quality, meteorology, background concentrations, receptor populations and documented exposure-response assumptions.

      Decision use. Compare backup and siting options through exposure analysis; prioritize monitoring near sensitive receptors and avoid attributing individual illness without evidence. Tradeoff or limitation: Carbon-oriented scheduling or backup substitution can move local pollutant exposure; modeled health damages are not observed diagnoses or proof of causation. Current coverage: Health studies provide modeled burdens and research gaps; this dataset contains no causal epidemiology or campus-specific verified exposure series.

      Residential noise and tonal character — LAeq/Lmax dBA; LCeq dBC; spectrum and tonality

      Purpose and boundary. Persistent tones and low-frequency sound can matter beyond a single average decibel value; facility-internal measurements do not describe residential exposure. Outdoor and relevant indoor residential receptors exposed to cooling, generators and construction; specify background conditions, time periods and equipment operation. [69] [27] [13] [23]

      Measurement. Measure calibrated A- and C-weighted levels, frequency spectra and tonal characteristics; compare baseline and operating periods under documented meteorology. Suggested cadence: Collect day and night baselines, commissioning measurements and seasonal operating surveys; investigate complaints and intermittent generator events with time-stamped records. Minimum evidence: Receptor map, calibrated instruments, LAeq and maximum periods, LCeq, frequency bands, background subtraction, meteorology, runtime and applicable local criteria.

      Decision use. Assess design mitigation and actual neighbor experience against verified local criteria; do not import occupational limits as residential standards. Tradeoff or limitation: Quieter cooling or barriers can affect energy use, airflow and cost; acoustically equal levels can have different perceived characteristics. Current coverage: Community studies identify concerns; no standardized Indiana or California receptor measurements are included, so compliance or health effects cannot be scored.

      Effluent heat and chemistry — m³/day; °C/temperature rise; pH; mg/L and kg/day

      Purpose and boundary. Returning water does not eliminate impacts if temperature, salts, biocides or other constituents affect treatment systems or aquatic ecosystems. Campus wastewater, cooling blowdown and receiving treatment or water bodies; distinguish sewer design capacity from measured outfall quality and quantity. [70] [56] [2]

      Measurement. Pair flow with temperature and chemical sampling to calculate loads; evaluate receiving-water conditions, treatment performance and source-specific chemicals used. Suggested cadence: Follow applicable permit schedules; proposed assessment adds representative seasonal and upset-event samples, with continuous flow and temperature where feasible. Minimum evidence: Actual flow, sampling location and method, temperature, chemistry, chemical inventory, receiving conditions, treatment destination, laboratory quality controls and applicable permit.

      Decision use. Evaluate cooling and wastewater designs using measured loads and receiving conditions; obtain the specific permit before assessing compliance or limits. Tradeoff or limitation: Water reuse can reduce withdrawal while concentrating constituents and increasing treatment demand; disposal pathways determine where remaining burdens occur. Current coverage: Indiana sewer approval supplies design flows only; no campus discharge temperature, chemistry or receiving-water monitoring is available in this dataset.

      Land change and biodiversity — Hectares disturbed; habitat condition and connectivity indicators

      Purpose and boundary. A small campus footprint can depend on extensive infrastructure or affect connected habitats; hectares alone omit ecological condition and sensitivity. Campus, substations, transmission, generation and relevant supply-chain land; distinguish previously developed land, sensitive habitats and indirect infrastructure changes. [71] [67] [9] [22]

      Measurement. Compare mapped pre-project land cover with surveyed habitat and post-project conditions; report fragmentation and species-specific findings without collapsing ecological differences. Suggested cadence: Establish a preconstruction baseline, monitor construction and restoration, then survey appropriate ecological seasons after operation and major site changes. Minimum evidence: Project and associated infrastructure maps, baseline land cover, seasonal habitat surveys, connectivity assessment, mitigation commitments and independently checked restoration outcomes.

      Decision use. Compare siting alternatives and avoidance measures using habitat evidence; keep biodiversity conclusions distinct from greenhouse-gas or fiscal performance. Tradeoff or limitation: Renewable generation and grid upgrades can reduce operational carbon while expanding land demand; restoration pledges require location-specific outcome verification. Current coverage: The present dataset lacks parcel-level ecological baselines or infrastructure footprints; this domain remains an unfilled project-assessment requirement, not zero impact.

      Hardware turnover, e-waste and critical materials — Tonnes/year by route; lifetime years; material-specific kg

      Purpose and boundary. Rapid hardware replacement increases manufacturing demand and waste; critical-material recovery and safe handling depend on traceable downstream processes. Servers, accelerators, storage, network equipment and batteries from procurement through reuse, recycling and final disposal, including material supply chains. [10] [19] [67] [22]

      Measurement. Reconcile asset and mass inventories; track service life, reuse destination and recovery by material, distinguishing collection claims from verified recycling outcomes. Suggested cadence: Record procurement and retirement events, audit downstream transfers annually, and update material inventories when equipment configurations or suppliers change. Minimum evidence: Asset register, equipment mass and composition, actual lifetimes, refurbishment records, waste manifests, downstream destinations and evidence of material recovery.

      Decision use. Choose repair, refurbishment and procurement options using life-cycle comparisons; require downstream documentation and material-specific reporting for retired assets. Tradeoff or limitation: Extending hardware life can reduce manufacturing demand while increasing operational energy; recycling rates alone can conceal losses of specific critical materials. Current coverage: Generative-AI research supplies global waste scenarios; no local asset mass balance or verified supplier and recycler chain is included.

      Cumulative burden and distributional equity — Separate exposure indicators by population and geography

      Purpose and boundary. A project-wide average can hide disproportionate burdens on particular neighborhoods or workers, while benefits may accrue to different populations. Nearby residents, workers and upstream communities, including sensitive populations; compare distribution of burdens and benefits within a defined assessment geography. [82] [12] [22] [23]

      Measurement. Overlay measured or modeled exposures with demographic vulnerability and existing burdens; involve residents and preserve each indicator rather than inventing a composite. Suggested cadence: Assess before siting, update annually with disclosed data vintages, and review when exposures, population conditions or expansion plans materially change. Minimum evidence: Spatial exposure estimates, background burden, population and worker characteristics, data vintages, community input, geographic uncertainty and separately reported benefits.

      Decision use. Identify priority monitoring and mitigation areas through source-specific analysis and community participation; avoid transferring California percentile ranks to Indiana. Tradeoff or limitation: Low statewide carbon can coexist with local pollution or water hardship; screening percentiles do not prove project-attributable illness or individual exposure. Current coverage: CalEnviroScreen 5.0 supports California screening, but no campus-to-tract overlay or community exposure inventory has been completed in this dataset.

      Public costs, jobs and fiscal distribution — Inflation-stated dollars; job-years; permanent FTE; rate effects

      Purpose and boundary. Large announced investment does not establish net community benefit; infrastructure costs and financial risks can shift among customers and taxpayers. Public budgets, utility customers, workers and host community over the project life; distinguish private investment from net local public benefit. [48] [49] [36] [34] [38]

      Measurement. Compare verified taxes and payroll with incentives, infrastructure, services and stranded-cost scenarios; separate construction job-years from ongoing full-time employment. Suggested cadence: Assess before agreements, verify annual realized jobs and revenue, and stress-test delay, closure or lower-than-promised demand throughout contract periods. Minimum evidence: Executed agreements, incentive schedules, verified payroll and employment, actual tax receipts, upgrade costs, financing terms and utility cost-allocation evidence.

      Decision use. Evaluate benefit agreements and utility cost allocation using common fiscal assumptions, distribution tables and documented responsibilities if projects underperform. Tradeoff or limitation: Jobs and revenue may coexist with environmental burdens; monetizing selected harms cannot replace separate health, water or biodiversity assessment. Current coverage: Indiana announcements provide job and tax commitments; tariff examples show allocation mechanisms, but no independent project net-benefit calculation is available.

      Disclosure, verification and accountability — Coverage percentages; reporting lag; unresolved cases and resolution time

      Purpose and boundary. Comparisons fail when reporting omits campuses or changes definitions; clear evidence status prevents promises and design permits becoming apparent measurements. Every reported metric, campus boundary and affected community; assess data completeness and accountability separately from environmental performance itself. [17] [12] [28] [38]

      Measurement. Track missing data, measurement versus estimation, assurance scope, public access and complaint outcomes; preserve versioned sources and correction histories. Suggested cadence: Update a public ledger quarterly where feasible, reconcile annually, and disclose material boundary changes, incidents and corrections promptly. Minimum evidence: Metric definitions, coverage denominator, data owner, reporting dates, raw-to-reported reconciliation, estimation flags, independent review scope and complaint-response records.

      Decision use. Set procurement and community reporting expectations, identify evidence gaps, and assign responsible owners without treating disclosure quality as low environmental impact. Tradeoff or limitation: More reporting improves scrutiny but has costs and confidentiality constraints; redaction should preserve decision-relevant aggregates and transparent explanations for gaps. Current coverage: This review preserves source status and uncertainty; project-level reporting coverage, regular complaint logs and independent assurance remain incomplete.

      Useful AI service and rebound — kWh/accepted task; service quality; total annual footprint

      Purpose and boundary. Lower energy per request can coincide with higher total demand; comparing unequal tasks or unconstrained output lengths can mislead procurement. Equivalent end-user service across training, tuning, inference, idle capacity and associated equipment; include deployment volume and any claimed downstream benefit. [6] [4] [8] [9] [17]

      Measurement. Measure energy for fixed quality and latency requirements, then multiply by actual volume; test counterfactual savings and induced additional demand. Suggested cadence: Benchmark before selection, remeasure after model or hardware changes, and review monthly service volume and annual operational and embodied totals. Minimum evidence: Representative task set, acceptance criteria, model and hardware versions, tokens, batch size, latency, energy boundary, utilization and annual workload volume.

      Decision use. Procure the least-burden option meeting service requirements, monitor absolute budgets, and report avoided impacts separately with a defensible baseline. Tradeoff or limitation: Smaller models, caching and batching may change quality, privacy or latency; efficiency gains may stimulate demand that erodes aggregate savings. Current coverage: Published benchmarks and BLOOM illustrate bounded cases; this dataset cannot rank current vendors or calculate a universal footprint per prompt.

      The dictionary is a synthesis of referenced methods and analyst recommendations. Monitoring frequencies are suggested practice unless a cited jurisdiction-specific instrument imposes a schedule. Significance thresholds require site context, an applicable permit or law, and explicit policy judgment. The twenty indicators are not weighted into one environmental score: lower carbon cannot automatically compensate for a local water shortage, pollution exposure, habitat loss or inequitable cost shift.

      11. Application to California policy and the CivicShield concept

      California’s new data-center legislation was signed in September 2026. Enactment is separated here from ordinary effective dates, agency implementation deadlines and project triggers. The policy register below links the required or decision-relevant measurements to their authority and limitations. It is a research mapping, not a determination that a particular project complies. [86]

      CEC forecasting, benchmarking and generation review

      Existing programs. California electricity planning, covered buildings and onsite thermal generation. IEPR forecasts incorporate utility data-center applications. Covered buildings over 50,000 square feet report annual energy use. Thermal generation of 50 MW or more falls within CEC licensing jurisdiction. [72]

      Assessment application. Connect load forecasts with procurement, local permitting and backup-generator impacts. Boundary: CEC does not permit data centers themselves. A small-power-plant exemption still requires environmental review and subsequent permits. Research question: How do projected connections compare with realized demand and local pollution?

      AB 1577: facility energy reporting

      Enacted; effective 2027-01-01. Data centers ≥10 MW; specified public, research and utility facilities excluded. Requires energy, peak-load, PUE and fuel reporting; CEC sets filing timing. Public releases are anonymized aggregates; IEPR load-trend assessments start in 2029. [73]

      Assessment application. Local discretionary applications must estimate electricity, onsite generation and boundary noise. Boundary: Facility reporting does not isolate AI workloads; customer energy data remain protected. Research question: Can aggregate disclosures reveal infrastructure pressure while retaining useful local accountability?

      AB 2469: water conditions for development

      Enacted; effective 2027-01-01. Construction or expansion increasing maximum peak water use; discretionary and ministerial approvals. Requires water-supply assessment, projected use, efficiency and workforce disclosures, and applicant funding of necessary water infrastructure. Water-scarcity plans begin January 1, 2028. [75]

      Assessment application. Evaluate dry-year reliability alongside project demand and infrastructure costs. Boundary: No MW cutoff appears here; satisfying conditions does not require approval. Research question: Are peak cooling demands compatible with enforceable drought reductions and existing customers' needs?

      AB 2619: water-use disclosure

      Enacted; effective 2027-01-01. Data centers across defined size classes, including facilities below 2 MW. Initial licensing requires good-faith water estimates and source disclosure; renewals report prior-year water use, direct use by source and cooling type. [76]

      Assessment application. Supplier shortage planning must include unconstrained demand from data centers and other large users. Boundary: Withdrawals or deliveries are not automatically consumptive losses or upstream electricity water use. Research question: How accurately do license estimates predict metered seasonal demand?

      SB 886: interconnection and cost protection

      Enacted; effective 2027-01-01. Participating data-center customers; transmission-specific provisions cover new agreements from January 1, 2027. Requires tariffs or updated rules by January 1, 2028 to prevent stranded costs and cost shifts. Transmission provisions allocate upgrade costs, constrain refunds and require termination protection. [77] [74]

      Assessment application. Compare committed demand, infrastructure exposure and demand-response participation. Boundary: Implementation requires CPUC decisions; transmission cost allocation is constrained by federal law. Research question: Do deposits and exit obligations cover realistic cancellation and underutilization scenarios?

      AB 2383: service tariffs and procurement

      Enacted; effective 2027-01-01. Participating customers; generation-tariff thresholds must be set no higher than 25 MW. Requires cost-protective service tariffs, clean-energy-consistent procurement and generation payment mechanisms lasting at least ten years. CCA/provider generation tariffs are due January 1, 2028. [74]

      Assessment application. Link added load with incremental procurement, collateral and societal-cost contributions. Boundary: Generation-specific exemptions and thresholds are not universal exemptions from every provision. Research question: Which contract terms best prevent stranded procurement without weakening clean-energy requirements?

      SB 1168: rate-structure assessment

      Enacted; effective 2027-01-01. Data-center transmission and distribution connections in CPUC proceedings. Requires assessing opportunities for rates that assign reasonable network costs and proportionate load-growth procurement costs to data centers while relieving residential rate pressures. [79]

      Assessment application. Connect resource planning to distributional effects on households, including CARE and FERA participants. Boundary: The act directs assessment; it neither publishes a completed tariff nor establishes a numerical cost allocation. Research question: How do allocation choices affect low-income customers under different load-growth outcomes?

      SB 887: CEQA review and conditional streamlining

      Enacted; effective 2027-01-01. Data-center entitlements; categorical-exemption restrictions contain specified facility exceptions. Extends CEQA coverage and bars categorical exemptions for covered projects. Leadership-project streamlining requires demanding energy, water, cost-protection and community-benefit conditions. [78]

      Assessment application. Streamlining conditions include hourly zero-carbon supply within five years, predominantly new resources, and efficient cooling. Boundary: These streamlining conditions are not universal operating standards for every data center. Research question: Do enforceable project conditions reduce cumulative burdens beyond statutory minimum review?

      SB 57: earlier cost-shift assessment

      Enacted; effective 2026-01-01. Electrical-corporation costs caused by new data-center loads. Authorizes assessment of procurement, network investment and stranded-asset cost shifts. Any completed assessment must be submitted to legislative committees and posted by January 1, 2027. [80]

      Assessment application. Provides an analytical bridge between electricity growth, infrastructure risk and ratepayer protection. Boundary: SB 57 authorizes assessment; it does not itself order a new tariff. Section sunsets January 1, 2031. Research question: Which documented cost risks warrant specific safeguards in later tariff proceedings?

      AB 93: vetoed water proposal

      Vetoed; no effective date. Historical data-center water-policy proposal. The Governor returned AB 93 without approval. It must not be displayed as an operative water-reporting mandate. [81] [75] [76]

      Assessment application. Compare the unsuccessful 2025 proposal with the separately enacted 2026 water-disclosure laws, AB 2469 and AB 2619. Boundary: A vetoed proposal supplies legislative context, not compliance authority; later bills require independent scope and effective-date checks. Research question: What changed between the vetoed proposal and the enacted water protections?

      CalEnviroScreen 5.0: cumulative-burden screening

      Final tool; not a facility permit. California census-tract pollution burden and population vulnerability. Updates screening data and methods, adding diabetes prevalence and small air-toxic sites. It informs CalEPA's final September 18, 2026 disadvantaged-community designation. [82]

      Assessment application. Screen proposed sites, then investigate nearby receptors, emissions, noise and community priorities. Boundary: A screening percentile neither measures a project's incremental health risk nor determines permit approval. Research question: Would the project add burdens where exposure and vulnerability already overlap?

      DWR: supplier shortage and drought planning

      Existing annual reporting. Urban suppliers exceeding 3,000 connections or 3,000 acre-feet annually. Requires annual supply-demand assessment and shortage reporting, generally by July 1; specified imported-water allocation timing can extend submission. [83]

      Assessment application. Use supplier assessments, shortage-contingency plans and drought-risk assessments to contextualize new cooling demand. Boundary: Regional or statewide sufficiency does not establish site-specific supply. Assessments require ongoing updates as actual conditions change. Research question: What restrictions apply when facility demand peaks during a supplier shortage?

      SB 253: corporate emissions disclosure

      Enacted statute; subsequent regulatory approval unverified. U.S. entities doing business in California with annual revenue above $1 billion. Requires Scope 1–2 reporting in 2026 and Scope 3 from 2027. CARB's September notice identifies November 10, 2026, conditional on OAL approval. [84]

      Assessment application. Use corporate emissions to supplement facility and supply-chain evidence. Boundary: Corporate totals are not facility or AI-model measurements. This review did not establish whether OAL approved the regulation after CARB’s September 1 notice. Research question: Can corporate inventories be reconciled with location-specific electricity and equipment emissions?

      California’s EPRI historical model estimate is 9.058 TWh in 2024, with source-variation bounds of 8.758–9.357 TWh and modeled peak demand of 1.069 GW. The 2030 low, medium and high scenarios are 10.793, 15.027 and 19.824 TWh. These are data-center-plus-cryptocurrency estimates, not measured AI loads or confidence intervals. No California campus appears in the harmonized Google water sample, so it cannot supply a California water total or WUE. A state electricity number also cannot determine local groundwater, air quality, noise or health outcomes. [25] [26] [28]

      The Indiana application reaches a parallel conclusion about incompatible boundaries: a campus annual water projection, a sewer design peak, municipal plant capacity and construction dewatering do not form a measured consumption series. The correct next step is a shared facility and utility inventory with monthly source-resolved balances, hourly hot-weather peaks, baseline wells, discharge and construction pumping kept distinct. The dashboard’s application view records the values supported by current evidence and the additional data needed. [45] [56] [43] [46]

      The supplied June 2026 CivicShield WIA and NAWEI artifacts adapt environmental review into workforce governance. Their useful components are purpose and need, alternatives, affected tasks, consultation, assigned mitigation and monitoring. They contain no measured physical footprint. Their unvalidated score weights differ between the plan and prototype, and the prototype’s multiplication of composite risk by worker count does not establish exposed or displaced workers. These are findings from the supplied design artifacts, not claims about a published validated instrument.

      CivicShield crosswalk: Purpose, baseline, alternatives

      Use equal service volume and quality across alternatives. Compare baseline versus deployment changes in task-hours, employment, earnings, service outcomes, energy, emissions, and water. Interpretive limit: The baseline and counterfactual must be documented; current staffing is not automatically the future without AI.

      CivicShield crosswalk: Exposure and consequence

      Separate hazard, exposure, severity, likelihood, duration, and reversibility. Record affected task-hours and workers independently from displacement, wage loss, safety, and service failures. Interpretive limit: Exposure is not displacement; a composite score is not a probability.

      CivicShield crosswalk: Tiered review

      Add documented significance criteria and escalation triggers for concentrated, irreversible, cumulative, or uncertain harms. Permit human escalation regardless of aggregate score. Interpretive limit: Bands are proposed governance choices, not validated thresholds or legal clearance.

      CivicShield crosswalk: Distribution and cumulative burden

      Evaluate repeated deployments across employers and regions, overlapping transition burdens, affected communities, and unequal access to training or clean infrastructure. Interpretive limit: Protect privacy and report subgroup uncertainty; regional correlation does not prove individual harm.

      CivicShield crosswalk: Mitigation and residual effects

      Compare pre-control and residual impacts; track implementation, worker retention, real earnings, placement quality, and environmental reductions against explicit targets. Interpretive limit: Training participation is not verified employment recovery; modeled improvement is not achieved mitigation.

      CivicShield crosswalk: Lifecycle physical footprint

      Add training and inference energy, electricity emissions, direct and indirect water consumption, hardware manufacturing, replacement, and waste; identify site, time, allocation, and supplier evidence. Interpretive limit: Avoid double counting; report absolute totals and useful-service intensity separately.

      CivicShield crosswalk: California policy and public accountability

      Develop pilot procurement disclosures, alternatives memoranda, accountable mitigation commitments, public monitoring, and reassessment when scope changes. Interpretive limit: These are policy options. The pitch's N-6-26 reference and any legal requirements require current primary-source verification.

      For procurements governed by California’s State GenAI procurement policies, a completed SIMM 5305-F risk assessment is required regardless of risk level, as clarified by CDT Technology Letter 24-03. A proposed environmental and workforce annex could add equal-service alternatives, attributable vendor resource data, hosting locations, lifecycle allocation, worker outcomes, absolute budgets, accountable controls and reassessment after material changes. This annex is a recommendation; it is not an existing universal mandate, a replacement for CEQA or permits, or a claim that NAWEI scores satisfy California procurement requirements. [85]

      A useful pilot compares the current workflow, process redesign, conventional software, a smaller model and the proposed system using the same service target. Collect task-hours, retention and inflation-adjusted earnings separately from electricity, lifecycle emissions and seasonal water. Measure actual follow-up outcomes rather than translating abstract risk scores into predicted headcounts. Report any external societal emissions savings against a separate counterfactual and evaluate whether increased demand uses up operational efficiency gains. [9] [17] [19]

      Priority implementation research should test whether California reporting improves independent reproducibility; whether tariff and contract rules allocate realized costs as intended; whether project disclosures capture cumulative local burdens; and whether AI procurement pilots deliver useful service with lower absolute physical impacts and acceptable worker outcomes. Predefine outcomes, comparison groups, confounders, uncertainty and material changes that reopen review.

      Four distinct measurement streams.

      AIR & NOISE

      Exposure where people live

      PM₂.₅ (µg/m³), appropriately measured NO₂, daytime and nighttime sound (dBA; specified averaging period), weather and event logs. Place monitors using wind, terrain and other sources—not distance alone.

      WATER

      Availability as well as use

      Withdrawal and consumption (m³) separately; source, basin, well levels or service pressure where relevant. Track drought, season and other users. A water-use total alone does not measure household access.

      COMMUNITY HEALTH

      Outcomes with denominators

      Agency-provided asthma emergency visits or hospitalizations with consistent geography, periods and population denominators. Validated sleep and symptom surveys require a separate, approved participant protocol. Complaints are signals, not disease rates.

      CLIMATE & PUBLIC COST

      Keep accounting separate

      Electricity and fuel emissions (tCO₂e), utility and infrastructure costs, and who pays. Track power-generation impacts beyond the host community. Do not combine dollars, carbon, water and illness into a severity score.

      Study design: what makes before-and-after credible?
      1. Agree on the question before collecting. Residents, public-health scientists and agencies should prespecify primary outcomes, exposure pathways, geography, reporting cadence and the changes the study can detect. Include renters, Tribal partners where relevant, and people with existing illness.
      2. Select comparison areas carefully. Assess prior trends, population, healthcare access, industry and socioeconomic conditions. Check for pollution or grid impacts spilling into the comparison area. Multiple sites may be needed.
      3. Measure concurrently through each phase. A suggested 12-month environmental baseline captures seasonality; it is a starting design choice, not a universal minimum. Use longer historical health series where available. If building has begun, label reconstructed baseline data as retrospective and disclose gaps.
      4. Analyze changes with uncertainty. Plan comparative interrupted time series or difference-in-differences with pretrend checks, seasonal and weather adjustment, time correlation and sensitivity analyses. Account for wildfire smoke, traffic, pollen, other development and population change. A statistician must assess power; no sample size is assumed here.
      5. Separate signals from conclusions. Small populations and rare outcomes may prevent reliable estimates. A non-significant finding does not establish safety. Do not infer new cancer or mortality effects from a short follow-up. Publish missingness, uncertainty and null findings.
      6. Include maintenance workers through a separate occupational protocol. Generator work, heat, noise and chemical exposure differ from residential exposure. Protect worker confidentiality and employment rights; do not pool workers and residents into one risk estimate.
      Measurement quality and a response plan

      Write a quality-assurance project plan: purpose, responsibilities, instrument suitability, detection limits, placement, calibration, reference-monitor collocation, maintenance, completeness targets and data review. Preserve raw values and correction versions; flag suspect readings rather than silently deleting them. Low-cost sensors are not automatically regulatory monitors or diagnostic devices.

      Agree with local health and environmental agencies on how unusual readings are verified, when residents are notified and who investigates. Do not wait for statistical significance to respond to a credible hazard. Record incidents and remedial action independently of the research analysis.

      Community control, consent and privacy before enrollment

      This dashboard is a planning and evidence interface, not a clinical intake system. These downloads contain blank environmental and project-event fields only. Do not upload names, symptoms, medical records, exact home addresses or participant identifiers to the project ledger.

      Appoint a community advisory group and an accountable study sponsor. An institutional or public-health authority must determine applicable research oversight, consent and data-sharing requirements; community monitoring is not automatically exempt human-subject research. Establish secure restricted storage, role-based access, retention rules and audit trails before participant collection.

      Publish only reviewed environmental data and disclosure-controlled health aggregates. Small cells, overlapping tables and fine locations can identify people even without names. Apply the data provider’s suppression rules, including complementary suppression where needed. Operator funding should support independent monitoring without control over analysis or publication.

      At home: duration and timing matter

      Ask for levels at homes, especially bedrooms at night—not only beside an engine. Separate continuous cooling fans from generator testing, outages and construction. An average can hide short peaks or a persistent hum.

      85 dBA is not a neighborhood standard. Occupational hearing guidance does not establish an acceptable residential noise level. NIOSH: workplace versus environmental noise

      At work: measure the whole shift

      NIOSH recommends an occupational exposure limit of 85 dBA averaged over 8 hours. Each 3 dBA increase halves the corresponding duration: 88 dBA / 4 hours; 91 dBA / 2 hours. Combine all exposures across the shift.

      Use personal dosimeters for maintenance staff and calibrated meters for equipment noise. Prioritize quieter equipment and engineering controls. NIOSH measurement guidance

      What a useful project noise report must show
      • Where and when: distance, microphone location, weather, time of day and generator load; measure baseline before construction.
      • Typical and loudest periods: LAeq (energy-average A-weighted level over a specified interval) and LAFmax (maximum A-weighted level using Fast response).
      • Hum and rumble: frequency-band results and tonal assessment, alongside dBA. A-weighting reduces the contribution of low frequencies.
      • Operating scenarios: ordinary cooling, individual tests, simultaneous tests and outage operation; state the number of engines running.
      • Accountability: applicable local limits, mitigation, independent checks and a complaint-response process.
