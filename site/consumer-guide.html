Data centers and your community

A practical guide to the cloud's local footprint

Data centers support everyday services, from searching photos to business software, science and hospital records. Their impacts depend on location, electricity supply, cooling and equipment. Choose tools that meet your needs; operators, utilities and officials make the infrastructure decisions. No single water-per-prompt number describes everyone's use. [2] [3] [5] [19]

How it works

1. Computers do the work

A data center houses computers that store information and run services. Your request joins many others. The electricity needed depends on the task, equipment and workload; different kinds of AI do not have one common footprint. AI is only part of data-center activity. [6] [39] [57]

2. Heat has to leave

Computers produce heat, so facilities need cooling. Cooling designs use different combinations of electricity and water. Some water returns to a water system; some is lost through evaporation. A design that reduces on-site water can still have impacts through its electricity supply. [2] [3]

3. The footprint extends beyond the fence

Power generation, equipment manufacturing and disposal also matter. A low-carbon electricity supply does not erase the impacts of making servers. Assessments need clear boundaries: a claim about cooling alone tells you less than an account covering the facility, purchased electricity and equipment life cycle. [4] [10] [19]

Around your home

Will my electricity bill go up?

There is no nationwide answer. Who pays for new infrastructure depends on utility rates and contracts. Oregon and AEP Ohio provide examples of special rules intended to assign costs to large data-center customers. Ask your utility who pays if a promised project uses less power than planned. [33] [34] [36]

Will there be enough water?

Annual gallons alone cannot answer that. Water source, dry-season demand, return flows and other users matter. Treatment capacity is also different from water reliably available during drought. Ask for local supply analysis and monthly operating data, with construction pumping reported separately from long-term cooling. [2] [3] [62] [43] [46]

Will I hear it from home?

Cooling equipment and generators can create noise. The useful question is what reaches nearby homes, including nighttime sound and recurring tones. Ask for measurements at residential locations, enforceable limits and a complaint process. Sound levels inside a facility do not describe a neighbor's exposure. [13] [23] [27]

What do we know about health?

Research identifies reasons to examine air pollution, noise and water conditions, but the reviewed studies do not establish that a particular facility caused an individual illness. Health models estimate possible population impacts. Local exposure measurements and ongoing monitoring are needed to assess a specific community. [13] [14] [23]

What could our community gain?

Potential benefits include useful computing, construction work, permanent jobs and public revenue. Indiana announcements describe jobs and school funding, but promises are not an independent assessment of net benefits. Compare permanent employment and public revenue with incentives, infrastructure costs and local environmental burdens. [48] [49] [9] [12]

Decode the claims

“100% renewable”

This may describe annual purchases or certificates matching electricity use. It does not demonstrate carbon-free power at every operating hour.

Evidence to request: Ask which sites and years are covered, how electricity is matched, and whether the claim reports local grid emissions alongside contractual accounting. [60] [61]

“Closed-loop cooling”

Water or another cooling fluid circulates within a system. That design label does not establish zero water use across the site and its supply chain.

Evidence to request: Ask how heat leaves the loop, what water is needed for filling and maintenance, and what actual site meters show. [2] [48]

“Low PUE”

The facility uses relatively little electricity beyond the computers themselves. This efficiency ratio does not tell you how much electricity the whole facility uses.

Evidence to request: Request total electricity, computer electricity, the reporting period and measured performance. Efficient facilities can still consume substantial electricity as computing grows. [1] [39]

“Water positive”

Ask what this particular pledge promises: reduced use, watershed projects or estimated replenishment. The label alone does not establish relief for local households.

Evidence to request: Request project locations, timing, methods and verified results, compared with the campus's withdrawals and consumptive losses in the affected watershed. [2] [62] [28]

“The permit says X million gallons a day”

Check what the number measures. In Indiana, an operator's 18-million-gallon annual projection and a 1.612-MGD sewer design peak describe different boundaries and time scales. Neither is measured campus consumption.

Evidence to request: Identify the specific project, average versus peak flow, construction versus operations, and actual meter readings. [45] [56]

“AI saved carbon”

A useful application may reduce emissions elsewhere, but that does not automatically make its computing emissions disappear or prove an overall benefit.

Evidence to request: Ask what would have happened without AI, how savings were measured, which computing and equipment impacts were counted, and whether increased activity offsets savings. [9] [4] [19]

Five questions for a public meeting

  1. Who pays for power and water upgrades if the project is delayed, shrinks or closes?

    The utility contract and cost-allocation terms. [36] [34]

  2. What happens to water supply during the hottest, driest months?

    Monthly withdrawals, consumptive losses, drought limits and the local supply assessment. [2] [62]

  3. What will nearby residents experience after opening?

    Baseline and operating noise measurements, generator hours, air monitoring plans and a named complaint contact. [13] [23] [27]

  4. Which community benefits are binding rather than aspirational?

    Permanent-job commitments, tax agreements, incentives, public costs and the consequences of missed commitments. [48] [49]

  5. What information will residents receive after approval?

    A public reporting schedule, independent checks, enforceable limits and the official responsible for resolving violations. [12] [17] [38]

Useful terms

Power (MW)

The rate of electricity use. One megawatt equals 1,000 kilowatts; capacity is not actual use. [26]

Energy (kWh)

Electricity used over time: one kilowatt running for one hour uses one kilowatt-hour.

Water withdrawal

Water taken from a source or supplied to a facility, before accounting for what returns. [2] [28]

Water consumption

Water not returned within the stated accounting boundary, often through evaporation. Check the boundary used. [2] [28]

MGD

Million US gallons per day: a flow rate, not an annual total or a storage volume.

PUE

Power usage effectiveness: total facility electricity divided by computer-equipment electricity. It has no units. [39]

WUE

Water usage effectiveness: commonly liters of site water per kilowatt-hour of computer electricity. Check what water is counted. [2]

CO₂e

Carbon dioxide equivalent: greenhouse gases expressed on a common warming scale, often in kilograms or tonnes. [60] [4]

Before the numbers

What kind of data center are we talking about?

A data center brings computers, storage and networking together with power, cooling and security. It can serve an organization’s own needs or supply computing to others. These labels describe different aspects of a facility:

Enterprise
An organization operates infrastructure for its own services, such as business systems or research.
Colocation
Customers place their equipment in shared premises. The operator supplies space and supporting infrastructure.
Cloud
A provider makes computing resources available remotely. This describes how a service is delivered.
Hyperscale
Large, scalable computing operations. This describes scale; it is not a universal server-count threshold or a synonym for every cloud facility.
Edge
Computing located closer to users or devices to reduce communication delay. Location alone does not establish a small environmental footprint.
Campus
A project site that may contain multiple buildings and development phases. One map marker need not mean one operating building.

Definitions: AWS infrastructure explainer and LBNL’s 2024 U.S. report. Ask which buildings, phases and equipment a project’s numbers include.

Is a data center the same thing as AI?

No. Data centers also support email, storage, business software, science and other digital services. AI includes systems that recognize patterns, make predictions or generate content. Training develops a model; inference uses it. Both can require electricity, and their shares vary with the service and how much it is used. Large models often use data centers, but some inference runs on phones or personal computers. That moves part of the work; it does not erase the device’s energy or manufacturing footprint. Inference energy research; documented on-device and server models.

Does more efficient AI mean a smaller total footprint?

It can reduce electricity per useful task, but total use also depends on how many tasks are performed, output quality, equipment and the power supply. Illustrative arithmetic: halving energy per task while tripling the number of tasks increases task electricity by 50%. Measure absolute energy, emissions and water alongside efficiency. Compare equal service quality and include equipment manufacturing when making a lifecycle claim. Green AI review; computing lifecycle research.

How should I read a project map or filing?

Check whether the record describes a zoning request, a proposed campus, a permitted component, construction or actual operations. A generator permit does not establish that an entire campus is operating. Generator capacity, computer capacity and total facility demand measure different things. In Project watch, inspect the document type, project phase, source and measurement boundary before comparing numbers. An opposition comment records a concern; establishing the impact requires supporting evidence.

Where do the numbers come from?

The denominator is the average monthly residential electricity consumption in EIA Table 5a, 2024 data (released October 7, 2025; checked October 1, 2026). Published values are rounded to whole kWh. Multiplying by 12 provides an annual comparison benchmark, not a seasonal household demand profile.

Capacity mode: annual MWh = facility MW × average fraction of capacity × hours in the selected year (8,760 normally; 8,784 in a leap year). Annual-total mode uses the entered MWh directly. Residential-customer equivalents = annual MWh × 1,000 ÷ (state monthly kWh × 12). No PUE factor is added to total-facility inputs.

For example, 100 MW at an average 80% in 2026 gives 700,800 MWh. Pennsylvania’s 2024 denominator is 817 × 12 = 9,804 kWh per customer-year, producing about 71,481 customer equivalents. This is a hypothetical example.
